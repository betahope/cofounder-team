# Customer interviews

How Maya helps a founder run a small round of customer interviews that changes a real decision. It builds on Maya's own guidance in SKILL.md (stay close to users, name what would change your mind). It adds two published sources from Y Combinator: Eric Migicovsky (YC partner, founder of Pebble), "How to Talk to Users", a Startup School lecture whose transcript YC published in "Startup School Week 1 Recap: Kevin Hale and Eric Migicovsky" on the YC blog (September 4, 2019); and Gustaf Alströmer (YC Group Partner), "How to talk to users", a Startup School video in the YC Startup Library (no date shown).

SKILL.md covers who Maya is and how she talks. This file covers the worked detail for one job: customer interviews that inform a product decision.

**What this is for, and what it is not.** These are learning conversations with current or potential users, run by the founder, to understand a problem before deciding what to build or change. It is not for selling on the call: pitching, asking for a pre-order or pilot, or handling objections is a sales conversation, and that sits with Jack Reeves. It is not for recorded testimonial videos for marketing, which Priya Sharma makes. The words customers use often help with messaging too. Share them with Jack, who owns positioning and messaging. Testing a clickable prototype is a separate session (a usability test), still Maya's, but not covered here.

Two modes:

- **Mode A: Run interviews step by step.** From the decision at stake to the call you make after five interviews.
- **Mode B: Review.** The founder brings a question list or interview notes, and Maya checks them against the lessons.

If the request is unclear, ask which mode.

---

## The core lessons (apply in both modes)

1. **Founders do the talking to users.** Do not hand it to a hire. Every founder takes part, the ones who code included. (Migicovsky)
2. **Do not pitch.** The call is for getting information out of the other person, not selling them on the product. Leave your idea out until the end of the call, or leave it out entirely, because it biases the answers. (Migicovsky, Alströmer)
3. **Ask about the past, not the future.** "Tell me about the last time this happened" gives you facts. "Would you use this?" gives you a polite yes that means nothing. (Migicovsky, Alströmer)
4. **Listen more than you talk.** Founders talk for a living. On this call, hold back, listen and take notes. (Migicovsky)
5. **Users have good problems and bad solutions.** Their job is to tell you about the problem. Yours is to decide the features. When someone asks for a feature, ask why until you find the problem behind it. (Alströmer)
6. **Look for what they already do about it.** If people are not already trying to solve the problem, it may not hurt enough for them to want your better answer. What they use today is also your real competition. (Migicovsky)
7. **Watch, do not only listen.** If they can share their screen and show how they handle it today, that beats any description. Behaviour is hard to change, so learn it in detail. (Alströmer)
8. **Throw out bad data.** Compliments ("I love it") and hypotheticals ("I'd probably use that") are not evidence. Steer back to specifics. (Migicovsky)
9. **Talk, do not survey.** A short video, phone or in-person conversation teaches more than a pile of survey responses. (Alströmer)
10. **Start small.** You do not need hundreds of interviews. Start with one, then three, then five. Your process gets better with every early call. (Migicovsky)

The times and counts below are these speakers' suggestions, not rules. Treat them as starting points.

## Mode A: Run interviews step by step

### Step 1: Gather the basics (one round of questions only)

Start from the company basics you already have (the company memory or company brief). Ask only for what you still don't know:

- The product (or idea), in one line
- Who the users are, or who the founder thinks they will be
- Stage: idea, prototype, or live with users
- When the founder last spoke to a user, and what they heard
- Anyone they could ask this week (customers, friends, former coworkers)

Never invent users, quotes, numbers or results. If something is missing, ask.

### Step 2: Name the one decision the interviews must inform

Agree on one decision before booking any call, such as "Do we build the reporting feature next?" or "Is this problem worth a company?". Then write down two things:

- **What we believe now.** The current answer and why.
- **What would change our mind.** The specific thing that, heard from several people, would flip the decision. For example, "If most of them already solve this with a spreadsheet they are happy with, we drop it."

If the founder cannot name a decision, the interviews will drift. Push for one.

### Step 3: Choose who to talk to and find five

Pick people who have the problem, not people who might like the product. At the prototype stage, look for the best first customer: someone for whom the problem costs real money, comes up often, and who has the budget and the authority to fix it. A daily user without budget may not be the buyer. (Migicovsky)

Ways to find five:

- **Start with yourself.** Walk through the last time you hit the problem. It tests the questions. (Migicovsky)
- **Warm contacts.** Friends, coworkers and former coworkers, and their introductions. Easy to reach, but friends may soften answers so as not to hurt you. (Migicovsky, Alströmer)
- **Where the users gather.** LinkedIn, Reddit, Slack or Discord communities, and in-person events. (Alströmer)
- **Turn up in person.** One YC company got dozens of short meetings by walking into fire stations unannounced. Industry events work too: meet people outside the hall, no booth needed. (Migicovsky)

Draft a short outreach message: who the founder is, any shared connection, that they are working on something new (one line, no pitch), and a request for a 20-minute call. (Alströmer) Run the message through the `humanizer` skill before the founder sends it.

### Step 4: Write questions about past behaviour, not opinions

Give the founder five to seven questions, each about something that already happened. Build them from these:

- "Tell me how you do [the task] today."
- "What's the hardest part about [the task]?"
- "Tell me about the last time that happened."
- "Why was that hard?"
- "How often does it come up?"
- "What, if anything, have you done to try to solve it?"
- "What don't you love about what you've tried?"

(Migicovsky, Alströmer) At the prototype stage, add questions that get numbers: what the problem costs them, how often it happens, and who holds the budget. (Migicovsky)

Keep follow-ups ready: "Tell me more about that." "What do you mean by that?" "Why is that important to you?" (Alströmer)

Cut any question that is a yes or no question, asks two things at once, or asks them to design the product: "Would you use this?", "Which features would make it better?", "What would a better version look like?" (Alströmer)

### Step 5: Run the call

- **Video, phone, or in person.** Not a form. (Alströmer)
- **Build some trust first.** They may tell you things nobody has asked them before. (Alströmer)
- **Keep it short.** A first conversation can be 10 to 15 minutes. Respect their time. (Migicovsky)
- **Ask, then listen.** Let them finish. Use the follow-ups.
- **Ask them to show you.** If they can share a screen, watch how they handle it today. (Alströmer)
- **Capture it.** Take detailed notes. If that is hard while talking, bring a co-founder to take notes, or ask permission to record. (Migicovsky)
- **No pitch.** If they ask what you are building, say you will share it at the end. Then keep it to a sentence.

### Step 6: Write notes within the hour, quotes word for word

Write up each call within an hour, while it is fresh. Use the same short template every time:

| Who (role, context) | What they do today | Hardest part, in their words | Last time it happened | What they have tried | Surprises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep quotes word for word, in the language the person used. Never tidy, translate or summarise a quote. Mark anything that is a compliment or a hypothetical so it does not count as evidence later. You won't know until later which detail matters, so write more rather than less. (Migicovsky)

### Step 7: Look for patterns after five

After five interviews, lay the notes side by side. Group what people said into buckets by problem (sticky notes or a simple table work), and see which problem comes up most and hurts most. (Alströmer) For each bucket, note how many of the five raised it unprompted and paste the strongest quote. Check the "what would change our mind" line from Step 2 against what you heard.

If the five disagree, or the right people were not in the group, run five more before deciding.

### Step 8: Decide

Make the call and say it in one line, tied to the decision from Step 2: build it, drop it, or change it. Give the two or three pieces of evidence behind it, with quotes.

Check the problem is worth solving: are people paying for other solutions today? Are they happy with a basic tool, such as a spreadsheet, that you would need to beat by a wide margin? How easy is this group to sell to? (Alströmer) Bring Jack in on that last question.

Then move fast. Turn the conclusion into the smallest thing you can test, and test it with the same people. (Alströmer) Record the decision in the company memory or brief, with the evidence that would change it.

## Mode B: Review a question list or interview notes

**For a question list**, check each question and show a table:

| Check | Pass / Fix | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Tied to one named decision | | |
| Asks about past events, not the future | | |
| No "would you use / buy this?" | | |
| No yes or no questions | | |
| One question at a time | | |
| Does not ask them to design features | | |
| Does not mention or pitch the idea | | |
| Asks what they already do about it | | |
| Short enough for 10 to 15 minutes | | |

**For interview notes**, check:

| Check | Pass / Fix | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Says who the person is and why they fit | | |
| Records what they do today, not only opinions | | |
| Has at least one past example in detail | | |
| Quotes are word for word | | |
| Compliments and hypotheticals marked, not counted | | |
| Records what they have tried and what they pay | | |
| Notes separate what they said from what the founder thinks | | |

Then give:

1. The top 3 fixes, in order of impact
2. For a question list, a rewrite of the weakest question. For notes, the key fact that is missing and the follow-up question that would get it.
3. A clear verdict. For questions: "Run these" or "Fix these first". For notes: "Enough to decide" or "Talk to more people first".

Don't nitpick. A good-enough list run this week beats a perfect one run next month.

## Rules

- Recommend one decision to inform and one next step. Don't list options and leave.
- Push back when the founder wants to pitch, or to ask "would you use it?".
- Push back when the founder wants to skip interviews and build. Ask when they last talked to a user.
- Never change a customer's words, and never run a quote through the humanizer.
- Never invent interview findings. Work only from notes the founder shares.
- If the founder wants to sell on the call, bring in Jack. If they want a testimonial video, bring in Priya.
