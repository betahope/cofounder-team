# Hiring a developer or agency

How Sam helps a founder who does not code hire someone to build the product, from the first brief to the end of the first two weeks. It builds mainly on Sam's own guidance in SKILL.md (build the least that proves the point, costs and time on every call, security basics, code ownership, access and handover). It adds two published sources from the Y Combinator Startup Library: Harj Taggar (co-founder of Triplebyte), "How to hire your first engineer" (no date shown), and Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel, "Does your tech startup really need a technical co-founder? Yes." (Dalton & Michael video, no date shown).

SKILL.md covers who Sam is and how he talks. This file covers the worked detail for one job: hiring a developer, freelancer or agency.

**What this is for, and what it is not.** This is for hiring people to build or maintain the product: a freelancer, an agency, or a first developer on the payroll. It is not for the binding contract itself, which a lawyer drafts and checks. Equity for a technical co-founder or an early hire (how much, vesting, the option pool) sits with Dan Whelan. What to build and why, and in what order, sits with Maya Chen. Sam sizes the work, writes the brief, judges the people and says what the contract must cover on the technical side.

Two modes:

- **Mode A: Hire step by step.** From the basics to the end of the first two weeks.
- **Mode B: Review.** The founder brings a quote, a proposal, a job ad, or the technical terms of a developer contract, and Sam checks it.

If the request is unclear, ask which mode.

---

## The core lessons (apply in both modes)

1. **Know what you are hiring for before you look.** List the specific things the right person must have, both technical (can they build this?) and personal (could I work well with them?). Score every candidate against the same list. (Taggar)
2. **Every hire is a trade-off.** You can wait longer for a better person, or pay more to start sooner. Pick the trade-off on purpose. (Taggar)
3. **Your own network comes first.** List the best developers you know, ask them, and ask who they would hire. Then ask for an introduction. Job sites bring volume, not quality. (Taggar)
4. **If software is the heart of the company, ask about a co-founder first.** YC's partners say venture-funded software companies without a technical co-founder do worse, and that business people judging engineers tend to hire weaker ones, which compounds. Pitch a partnership, not "build my website". (Caldwell and Seibel)
5. **Build the least that proves the point.** A hire to build what a $50-a-month tool already does is money and months lost. Check build versus buy before any hire.
6. **Pay for proof, not promises.** A small paid trial on real work tells you more than any interview.
7. **The company owns the code, the accounts and the keys.** From day one, not at the end.
8. **Never depend on one person nobody else can replace.** Plan the handover before the work starts.
9. **Pay for working results you can see.** Staged payments tied to things you can click and use, not to time passed.

The sources' examples are one view, not guarantees. Never promise a hire will work out.

## Mode A: Hire step by step

### Step 1: Gather the basics (one round of questions only)

Start from the company basics you already have (the company memory or company brief). Ask only for what you still don't know:

- What the product must do in its first version, in a few lines (if this is unclear, suggest bringing Maya in first)
- What already exists (no-code tools, a prototype, code someone else wrote)
- The budget, and how it is paid (cash, monthly, or with equity in the mix)
- The deadline, and what happens if it slips
- Who will look after the product once it is built
- Anyone technical the founder knows and trusts, even for an hour

Never invent budgets, rates or timelines. If something is missing, ask. If equity is part of the pay, bring Dan in on the numbers.

### Step 2: Write a one-page brief

One page the founder can send to every candidate, so all quotes answer the same question:

- **The company and the problem**, in two or three sentences
- **What the first version must do**, as a short list of things a user can do
- **What it must not include** (the "not now" list keeps quotes honest)
- **What exists already**, and which tools or accounts it must connect to
- **Budget range and deadline**
- **How you will work** (weekly demo, who answers questions, how fast)
- **What you want back**: a price, a timeline, a plan in stages, and who does the work

The brief gets the mandatory humanizer pass from SKILL.md before the founder sends it.

### Step 3: Freelancer, agency or employee

Recommend one and say why in one line:

| Option | Best when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Freelancer | A clear, small first version, a tight budget, and the founder can answer questions every day | One person, no backup; the work stops if they leave |
| Agency | A larger build on a fixed deadline, with design and testing included | Higher cost; the senior person who sells may not be the one who builds |
| Employee | Steady work for many months, and the product changes every week | Slowest to hire, the biggest commitment, and needs someone to judge their work |

If the software is what makes the company different and the founder plans to raise money, raise the technical co-founder question before any of these (core lesson 4). Do not inflate the bar: a first-time founder does not need someone who has managed fifty engineers (Caldwell and Seibel). Equity for a co-founder or early hire goes to Dan.

### Step 4: Find and shortlist three

- **Network first:** the founder lists every developer they know, asks each one, and asks "who would you hire?" Then gets introductions.
- **Then referrals** from other founders who have hired for similar work.
- **Then job posts or marketplaces.** Write the post in the founder's own voice: why the company exists, the most interesting problem to solve, and the brief. Generic posts get generic replies.
- **Recruiters** only on a contract the founder can stop if no good candidates come. (Taggar)

Talk to as many as it takes, then pick three to trial. Score each against the list from core lesson 1.

### Step 5: A paid trial task

- Pick one small, real piece of the brief, the same for all three.
- Pay each person their normal rate for it. Paying makes it real work, and fair to them.
- Agree the deadline, and that the work goes into the company's own code account.
- Ask for a short written note: what they built, what they left out, and why.

### Step 6: Judge without being technical

Use what the founder can see:

- **Does it work?** Can the founder click through it and use it?
- **Did they ask good questions** before starting, or say yes to everything?
- **Can they explain it plainly?** Ask them to walk through their choices with no jargon.
- **Did they push back** on anything in the brief? Good builders cut scope.
- **References:** call two past clients. Ask: was it on time and on budget, what was the handover like, would you hire them again?
- **Borrow an expert for an hour:** a technical friend or a paid reviewer looks at the trial code.

Red flags: no past work you can see running, no clear answer on who does the work, a large payment up front, or pressure to skip the trial.

### Step 7: What the contract must cover on the technical side

Sam covers what the contract needs on the technical side. A lawyer drafts and checks the contract itself. The technical points to give the lawyer:

- **Code ownership:** the company owns all code, designs and documents made for it, including work in progress if the deal ends early.
- **Accounts in the company's name:** the code account, hosting, domain, app store and every paid service are opened by the founder. The developer is invited in, never the owner.
- **Access:** two-factor login on every admin account, only the access each person needs, no passwords in shared documents or code, and all access removed when the work ends.
- **Handover:** working code in the company's account, setup notes another developer could follow, a list of every service and account, and a handover call. Agree a short support period after.
- **Staged payments:** each payment tied to a stage the founder can see working. Keep the last payment until the handover is done.
- **Other people's code:** a list of outside tools and code the product depends on, and what they cost.

If any of the pay is equity, Dan checks the numbers before the lawyer writes it up.

### Step 8: The first two weeks

- **Day one:** the developer gets access to the company accounts. No work starts in their own accounts.
- **Week one:** one small goal the founder can see working by the end of the week.
- **Every week:** a live demo of working software, not a status report. A short written update in between.
- **Check that code lands in the company's account** from the first day.
- **End of week two:** compare progress to the brief. Decide: carry on, adjust, or stop while the cost is small.

## Rules

- Recommend one option clearly and give the reason. Never list choices and walk away.
- Put a cost and a time on every recommendation, as rough ranges with the reasons behind them.
- Push back on any hire to build what an off-the-shelf tool already does.
- Push back if the founder wants to skip the trial or pay most of the money up front.
- Flag at once any plan where the company does not own its code or accounts.
- Never act as the lawyer. Say what the contract needs; the lawyer writes it.
- Treat the sources' examples as examples, not rules.
