# Raising a first round

How Dan helps a founder raise a first round (pre-seed or seed), from deciding whether to raise at all to the first investor update. Based on three published sources: Geoff Ralston, "A guide to seed fundraising" (YC Startup Library, Y Combinator, no date shown); Paul Graham, "How to Raise Money" (paulgraham.com, September 2013); and Y Combinator's SAFE page and forms (ycombinator.com/safe, where ycombinator.com/documents now leads).

SKILL.md covers who Dan is and how he talks. This file covers the worked detail for one job: raising a first round.

**Freshness rule.** Round norms change: typical round sizes, valuation caps, dilution, SAFE versions and how long a raise takes all move. Both essays are years old, and their numbers come from Silicon Valley. Wherever this file and a live source (or the founder's lawyer) disagree, trust the live version, and tell the founder that is what you are doing. Norms also differ between Ireland, the UK, the rest of the EU and the US. When that matters, say so and point the founder to local investors or counsel. Do not fill the gap with a guess.

**What this is for, and what it is not.** This is for planning and running the round: whether to raise, how much, on what terms, from whom, and how. The deck itself (slide structure, layout, slide copy) goes to the `pitch-deck-coach` skill; Dan shapes the investor story and what each slide needs to say. Legal paperwork (drafting and signing the SAFE or share documents, filings, binding terms) goes to a founder-friendly startup lawyer in the company's country. Deck visuals and demo videos are Priya's. The traction story is shared with Jack, who owns the GTM numbers behind it. Product metrics come from Maya, and the technical costs and hiring plan in the model come from Sam. Tax and filings go to an accountant. Accelerator applications go to the `startup-application-coach` skill.

Two modes:

- **Mode A: Plan and run the round.** Step by step, from the five facts to the first update.
- **Mode B: Review.** The founder brings a round plan, an investor list or an outreach email, and Dan checks it against the lessons.

If the request is unclear, ask which mode.

## The core lessons (apply in both modes)

1. **Raise only if you want it and it wants you.** Graham's rule. Outside money has to help you grow faster, and you have to be able to convince investors. Trying too early wastes time and burns your name with those investors.
2. **Be in fundraising mode or not.** Fundraising takes over the founders' minds. Pick a window, raise, then get back to work (Graham).
3. **One founder runs the raise.** Graham says the CEO. The others keep the company growing, because numbers that rise between meetings help close.
4. **Size the round to a believable plan.** Raise enough to reach the next fundable milestone, or profitability if you can. Ralston says that milestone is usually 12 to 18 months away. Have plans for different amounts (both sources).
5. **Talk to investors in parallel, best prospects first.** Graham calls it breadth-first search weighted by expected value: how likely an investor is to say yes, times how good a yes from them would be.
6. **The first commitment is the hardest.** After it, each close gets easier (both sources). Investors who decide fast and alone go first. Investors who say "we don't lead" go last.
7. **Hear no until you hear yes.** Only a definite offer with no conditions counts. Judge investors by what they do, and never leave a meeting without clear next steps (both sources).
8. **Valuation is not the main thing.** Graham's order: get the money you need, then good investors, then valuation "at best third". Ralston: do not over-optimise it.
9. **Use standard documents.** A standard SAFE keeps the talk to a few terms, often only the valuation cap (YC, Ralston).
10. **It is not done until the money is in the bank.** Sign and wire fast after a yes (both sources).
11. **Be nice, including to people who say no.** Graham: investors who said no after a close look are warm leads for the next round.

The figures in this file are examples from the sources, not guarantees or current market rates. Never promise a founder a valuation, a round size or a close date.

## Mode A: Plan and run the round

### Step 1: Gather the basics: the five facts (one round of questions only)

Start from the company basics you already have (the company memory or company brief). Ask only for what you still don't know, in one message:

- Runway: months of cash left at today's burn
- Monthly net burn
- The last milestone hit, with its date
- The next milestone, with a target date and a clear definition of done
- A cap table snapshot: founders, existing investors, option pool, outstanding SAFEs and notes
- If not known: the country the company is incorporated in, and where the founders plan to raise

Never invent numbers, investors or terms. If something is missing, ask.

### Step 2: Should you raise at all?

Give one answer (raise now, not yet, or don't raise) and the reason. Check:

- **Will money make you grow faster?** If not, Graham says don't raise.
- **Can you convince investors now?** Ralston: investors want a compelling idea, a team that can deliver it and a real, large market. Most also want a product and growth. He calls 10% a week for several weeks impressive. If the evidence is not there yet, his advice is "work on your product and talk to your users."
- **Default alive or default dead?** At today's burn, does the company reach profitability before the cash runs out? A plan that needs no raise is the strongest position to raise from (Graham).
- **Non-dilutive money.** Check grants and programs in the founder's country before or alongside the round. Name the scheme only when you know it applies.

If the answer is not yet, name the milestone that would change it.

### Step 3: How much, by milestone

- Start from the next fundable milestone. Count the months to reach it, plus a margin.
- Cost it: people, times monthly cost per person, times months, plus other costs. Both sources use $15k per person per month all-in, a Silicon Valley figure from years ago. Use the founder's real local costs instead.
- Build two or three plans (lowest, target, stretch) and say what each one buys. Investors ask how much you are raising partly to see if you fit the cheque size they like to write (Graham).
- Open with the lower plan. Graham: reaching an early target fast signals momentum, and you can raise more if interest holds.
- Dilution: Ralston says most seed rounds take up to 20% and to try not to exceed 25%. Graham (2013) said not more than 25% in that round, on top of under 15% sold earlier. Check what is normal now in the founder's market.
- Bring Jack in for the revenue assumptions and Sam for the technical costs and hiring plan.

### Step 4: SAFE and terms

- **Dan's default:** a pre-seed raised on SAFEs from smart angels, not priced. A priced round at seed when traction supports a real valuation conversation. Say what would change this (a strategic fund offering to lead, for example).
- **What a SAFE is (YC):** the investor funds now for the right to shares later. It converts automatically when the company raises a priced round. Usually the only term to negotiate is the valuation cap.
- **Post-money SAFE maths (YC):** pick the amount and the ownership you will sell, and the cap follows (amount divided by ownership). YC's example: $1M for 15% gives a cap of about $6.7M post-money. Run it with the founder's own numbers.
- **YC's US forms:** cap with no discount, discount with no cap, or uncapped with MFN, plus an optional pro rata side letter. Explain MFN (the SAFE takes the best terms of any SAFE issued later) and pro rata (the right, not the duty, to invest in the next priced round to keep the same share) the first time. YC says keep the SAFE unmodified and put any extra rights in a side letter.
- **No price set yet:** if an investor pushes for a valuation before deciding, Graham says talk first about whether they want to invest at all. For eager early investors he suggests uncapped with MFN, so the next investors set the cap.
- **Priced rounds** are more complex, costly and slow, and always need a lawyer (Ralston).
- **Outside the US:** YC publishes versions for Canada, the Cayman Islands and Singapore only, and tells founders to consult a lawyer licensed where the company was formed. For Irish, UK and other EU companies, the lawyer picks the instrument. Ask them whether it works with any tax relief the angels rely on (SEIS/EIS in the UK, for example).
- Model the conversion and show the founder the cap table after the round.

### Step 5: A target list of 50

Build a list of about 50 investors as a table:

| Investor | Type (angel, syndicate, pre-seed fund, seed fund) | Cheque and stage | Sector and geography fit | Leads or follows | Intro path | Beyond the cheque | Priority (A/B/C) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- Research what each one invests in and why (Ralston).
- Priority is expected value (Graham). Investors who decide fast and can commit alone go in A.
- Followers ("we don't lead") and investors who call themselves "valuation sensitive" go in C, contacted last (Graham).
- Ask of each one: what do they bring beyond the cheque? If nothing, move them down.
- Intro paths: the best comes from an investor who has just committed, the next from a founder they backed (Graham). Ask every new investor for intros. Cold email only when there is no path (Ralston).
- Platforms and crowdfunding are mostly for filling a round, not starting one (both sources).
- Never invent investor names, cheque sizes or portfolio companies. Mark any name Dan suggests "check" until the founder confirms it on the investor's own site.

### Step 6: Materials

- **Outreach email:** short. Who you are, what the company does, the strongest proof, what you are raising, and the ask (a meeting).
- **One-page summary.** Ralston lists vision, product, team (with location and contact), traction, market size and minimum financials (revenue, money raised before and now).
- **The deck:** a clear leave-behind. Hand the structure and slides to the `pitch-deck-coach` skill and the visuals to Priya. Dan checks the ask matches the round plan.
- **A demo.** Ralston calls demos almost a requirement.
- **A light model and a clean cap table.** Skip heavy diligence files at seed. Ralston: an investor asking for too much diligence is one to avoid.

Every email, summary and slide line gets the mandatory humanizer pass from SKILL.md before the founder sees it.

### Step 7: A six to eight week process

Run the raise as a project with a set window. Six to eight weeks is Dan's target, not a figure from the sources:

| Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|
| Before week 1 | List built, materials ready, intros lined up, pitch practised |
| 1 to 2 | First meetings, packed close together so talks run in parallel |
| 3 to 5 | Follow-ups and partner meetings, first commitments, intros from new investors |
| 6 to 8 | Close: roll up each yes, then stop |

- Track every investor: where they are, the next step and its date (Ralston: stay organised).
- Don't negotiate in the room. Take requests away and get help from advisors or the lawyer (Ralston).
- Take an acceptable offer when you have one (Graham). He calls a three working day deadline on an offer acceptable and a shorter one a warning sign.
- Keep the company growing during the raise.
- Stop when it stops working ("air in the straw", Graham). Then choose with the founder: cut burn, reach the next milestone, or raise a small bridge.

### Step 8: Close and first update

- After a yes, get the signature and the money fast, once the lawyer has checked the final documents. Ralston: a SAFE closes by signing online and sending the wire. Graham: chase each wire until it lands.
- Update the cap table and the model with what closed, not what was promised.
- Thank everyone, including those who said no, and keep them as warm leads.
- Send the first investor update soon after the close, then keep a set rhythm (monthly or quarterly). Cover what closed, the numbers against plan, the next milestone, any bad news, and one clear ask (intros, hires, customers).
- Record the round, and the next milestone it pays for, in the company memory or company brief.

1. The top 3 fixes, in order of impact
2. A rewrite of the weakest part (the ask line of the plan, the top five rows of the list, or the email itself)
3. A clear verdict: "Start the raise", "Fix these first" or "Don't raise yet"

## Rules

- Recommend one option and give the reason. Say what would change your mind.
- Push back on a raise with no milestone, an open-ended process, or a founder chasing the highest valuation.
- Never state a current valuation, cap, round size or dilution norm as fact. Use the founder's numbers and the sources' examples, labelled as examples.
- Never invent investors, terms or traction.
- Dan is not the company's lawyer. Every binding document goes to a startup lawyer in the company's country.
- When a point depends on Irish, UK, EU or US norms, say so and ask rather than guess.
